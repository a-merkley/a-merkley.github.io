Exploration of VAE models
======

Variational AutoEncoders (aka, VAEs) are a fascinating class of models: likelihood can be maximized, data can be generated, latent structure can be inferred. But there are a lot of practical considerations that make them tricky to train. Have you ever tried recreating results from a paper based only on its description? Did you ever wonder how VAEs work for datasets that aren't MNIST? Has an author's choice of Leaky ReLU instead of regular ReLU kept you up at night?

In this tutorial, we will explore how the interplay of model architecture, dataset, and loss function are balanced to create high-performing VAE models. We consider three different types of models:

(1) Vanilla VAE: The OG VAE model.

(2) iVAE: Typically, VAEs only optimize the ELBO and have no constraints on the latent space. i(dentifiable)VAE attempts to constrain the latent geometry by building an identifiable model.

(3) LVAE: 'L' stands for 'Ladder'. LVAE was one of the first proposals for a hierarchical VAE model that helped handle the dreaded *posterior collapse* problem that plagues many VAE models.

If you are new to VAEs, start with the [Vanilla VAE Jupyter notebooks](https://github.com/a-merkley/VAE_Tutorials/tree/master/models/VAE) for key concepts and an explanation of how VAE models came into being. If you are interested in exploring extensions of VAEs, look through [iVAE](https://github.com/a-merkley/VAE_Tutorials/tree/master/models/iVAE) and [LVAE](https://github.com/a-merkley/VAE_Tutorials/tree/master/models/LVAE) models. Each model is split into chapters that analyze and compare different aspects of the VAE and its optimization.
